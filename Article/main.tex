\begin{abstract}
A persistent security activation gap constrains IoT adoption: security mechanisms are widely available in commercial platforms yet remain inactive after deployment. We present a security-by-design methodology for centralized IoT ecosystems that treats identity as an intrinsic entity property, spans seven stages across four phases, and integrates cybersecurity throughout the development lifecycle. A dynamic Root of Trust process establishes verifiable cryptographic identity for each entity, the foundation for authentication, access control, and secure communication.

We validate the methodology through a confirmatory case study applying all seven stages to a DC Smart Microgrid: two ESP32 nodes and a Raspberry Pi~4B coordination agent. Four case study questions assess guidance sufficiency, identity model unambiguity, Root of Trust verifiability, and threat coverage. All seven stages produced documented, traceable artifacts with no external guidance required; a 14-parameter identity model across four entity types was produced without ambiguity; the methodology achieves unweighted OWASP IoT Top~10 coverage of 6/10 items fully and 4/10 partially addressed. The Root of Trust reconstructs each node's cryptographic identity from a Physically Unclonable Function response rather than a stored key, deriving its session key from an ephemeral ECDH exchange for forward secrecy; it was validated end-to-end on real hardware for both nodes, including a detailed $N=30$ hard-reset trial (30/30 success, median handshake latency 1300\,ms, mean $1313 \pm 48$\,ms) that an independent trial on the second node confirmed generalizes across physical chips. The methodology's parameterized, technology-agnostic structure is designed to generalize across diverse IoT domains; empirical validation in additional domains remains future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

The Internet of Things (IoT) has evolved from a conceptual vision into a technological paradigm underpinning critical infrastructure, industrial automation, healthcare systems, and smart cities \citep{al2015internet, zhou2021internet}. Despite this expansion, large-scale IoT adoption remains constrained by fragmented architectures, heterogeneous technologies, and---critically---the failure to integrate cybersecurity from the earliest design stages \citep{shahinzadeh2024security, makhdoom2018anatomy}.

The consequences of this gap are well documented. The Mirai botnet, which compromised over 600,000 IoT devices to launch unprecedented distributed denial-of-service attacks, originated from misconfigured or unprotected deployments relying on default credentials \citep{Mirai_analysis_2017}. Subsequent analyses confirm that cyberattacks on IoT infrastructure continue to escalate in frequency and sophistication, targeting energy systems, healthcare networks, and public utilities \citep{abraham2025cyber, okika2025addressing}.

A central observation motivating this work is that the principal barrier to secure IoT deployment is not the \emph{absence} of security features but the \emph{security activation gap}: the disconnect between security capabilities \emph{available} in a platform and those \emph{operational} after a standard deployment (Figure~\ref{fig:gap}). Widely adopted open-source IoT platforms provide security mechanisms that require manual post-installation configuration, effectively restricting their use to organizations with dedicated security expertise \citep{NIST_IoT_2020}.

A second, more fundamental barrier operates at the methodological level: most IoT development projects lack a structured process that integrates cybersecurity from organizational decision-making through operational maintenance. Security is typically addressed \emph{after} architectural and technology decisions are made, producing mechanisms that are bolt-on rather than foundational, with identity management that is platform-specific rather than entity-centric.

To address both barriers, we present two complementary contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A security-by-design methodology} for developing centralized IoT ecosystems. The methodology treats identity as an intrinsic property of every entity, spans seven stages across four phases (organizational strategy, architecture, implementation, and operation), and embeds cybersecurity---from threat policy to Root of Trust establishment---as a foundational concern from problem definition through ongoing operation. A dynamic Root of Trust (RoT) process establishes verifiable cryptographic identity for each entity before any operational communication begins.

    \item \textbf{An empirical confirmatory case study} applying the methodology to the design and implementation of a DC Smart Microgrid (SMG) IoT ecosystem, following Yin's \citeyearpar{yin2014case} Case Study Research design. The SMG comprises two ESP32 secondary control nodes and a Raspberry Pi~4B primary coordination agent, reaching Technology Readiness Level~3. Four case study questions evaluate guidance sufficiency, identity model unambiguity, Root of Trust verifiability, and OWASP IoT threat coverage.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Conceptual foundation:} The identity-centric IoT paradigm, the IoT/ecosystem distinction, the formal entity identity model, and the reconceptualization of the Root of Trust as a multi-phase process are established at the conceptual and definitional level in a companion manuscript \citep{campos2026identity}. The present work builds directly on that foundation to specify, validate, and empirically evaluate an operative seven-stage development methodology; it does not re-derive these definitions independently.

The present work evaluates the \emph{methodology} through the case study; implementation details are provided only to the extent required for methodological evidence.

We evaluate the methodology through: (a) stage-by-stage traceability, demonstrating that all seven stages produce documented, actionable artifacts; (b) quantitative measurements of identity provisioning and handshake latency; and (c) a systematic OWASP (Open Web Application Security Project) IoT Top~10 \citep{owasp2018iot} coverage analysis.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

We adopt the definition of IoT methodology from \citet{fortino2020internet}: a structured set of guidelines, methods, practices, and documents organized within a logical framework for solving the specific problem of IoT ecosystem development. We evaluate existing methodologies against nine criteria: the eight classical dimensions drawn from the literature, supplemented by a ninth, \emph{security validation}, which captures whether the methodology's security provisions are supported by empirical evidence from a formal case study or experiment.

Table~\ref{tab:metodologias} compares representative methodologies. A 2023 comparative study \citep{moreta2023comparison} confirms cybersecurity integration remains the most underdeveloped dimension across IoT development methodologies: most proposals emphasize functional aspects---interoperability, scalability, technological integration---while treating cybersecurity as an afterthought or omitting it entirely \citep{fortino2020internet, al2015internet, merzouk2020iot}. Agent-based approaches \citep{manate2014applying, ayala2015software, spanoudakis2015engineering} provide structured development processes but lack explicit security mechanisms and identity assignment; model-driven methodologies \citep{morin2017model, costa2016modeling, cicirelli2017metamodeling} address code heterogeneity and portability but not entity identity or security. A systematic review of agile IoT methodologies \citep{guerrero2023agile} confirms even recent structured approaches defer authentication, identity management, and threat modeling until after functional requirements are met---if addressed at all.

Security-focused proposals remain limited in scope and depth. \citet{josyula2017new} presents a security methodology for IoT systems engineering without specific guidelines for identity assignment or cryptographic mechanism selection. \citet{savaglio2018methodology} includes security aspects (authorization, key management, authentication) but not selection criteria or their timing relative to development stages. \citet{munoz2025internet} proposes a five-layer service-oriented architecture with security as a cross-cutting concern, without explaining how mechanisms are integrated or selected; its PKI-based scheme neglects resource-constrained devices. A systematic review of IoT authentication schemes in this journal \citep{saqib2023systematic} (over 120 proposals, 2010--2022) confirms core fragmentation: authentication mechanisms are proposed in isolation, without connection to a broader development process, formal identity model, or measurable threat coverage metric. \citet{blanco2023onto} propose an ontology-driven framework for cyber-physical security requirements, providing structured elicitation but not a full lifecycle spanning organizational policy through operational deployment.

More recently, several efforts target specific IoT security domains without holistic development guidance: Zero Trust architectures for industrial IoT \citep{zanasi2024flexible, fernandez2024critical}, blockchain-based supply chain security \citep{ray2024blockchain, fernando2023cyber}, and SDN-based IoT fog security \citep{javanmardi2023sdn, turner2023promising} address individual threat surfaces but not a development methodology. \citet{visintin2024secure} examines identity of things device-centrically but does not extend to ecosystem-wide identity management across heterogeneous entity types or connect identity to access control policy. PUF-based Root of Trust implementations \citep{sassalou2025puf} and hardware security modules \citep{bakiris12025iot} strengthen device-level identity but remain single-layer solutions, leaving the broader development process unaddressed. This work adopts an SRAM-based PUF for the ESP32, \texttt{esp32\_puflib} \citep{stanicek2022esp32puflib}, as the concrete Stage~4 technology for identity-parameter generation without persistent on-device key storage (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), integrated into the full seven-stage process rather than treated as an isolated device-level mechanism.

Standards from government agencies and consortia---Secure-by-Design \citep{ukgov2024sbdprinciples}, NIST IoT Cybersecurity Guidance \citep{NIST_IoT_2020}, ETSI EN~303~645 \citep{etsi_en303645}, the CSA IoT Security Controls Framework \citep{csa_iot_framework_2023}, and the 2025 NIST cybersecurity supply chain risk guidance \citep{licata2025developing}---provide valuable security baselines. However, these standards remain predominantly prescriptive at the policy level, offering no technology selection criteria, no process for deriving implementation choices from organizational constraints, and no deployment automation.

Taken together, these approaches share a common limitation: the absence of a methodology that simultaneously addresses (a)~organizational adoption strategy, (b)~technology-agnostic architectural design with a formal entity-centric identity model, (c)~a dynamic Root of Trust process establishing verifiable cryptographic identity, (d)~iterative development guided by maturity levels, and (e)~empirical validation through a formal case study with measurable artifacts at every stage. The methodology presented in this work addresses all five dimensions. The validation follows Yin's \citeyearpar{yin2014case} confirmatory case study design with four research questions and documented stage-by-stage artifact traceability---we are not aware of a comparable empirical validation of a complete IoT security development methodology using this research design.

\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:conceptual}
% ============================================================

Ambiguity in IoT definitions hinders coherent methodology design \citep{sorri2022revisiting}. Rather than re-deriving reference definitions, this methodology builds directly on the identity-centric conceptual framework established in a companion manuscript \citep{campos2026identity}, which reframes IoT as a technological paradigm in which cyber-physical systems are constitutively endowed with identity, and distinguishes this paradigm from an \emph{IoT ecosystem}---a digital environment in which entities possessing unique identities interact under a defined structure to provide services that solve identified problems. We adopt both definitions unchanged and use them as the entry point for the present methodology.

The ecosystem comprises seven element types: IoT devices (embedded systems with ecosystem-relative identity), IoT servers (network-connected infrastructure enabling entity communication), IoT services (processes enabling ecosystem operation and security), storage systems (information persistence infrastructure), users (business-logic-level entities with defined roles), administrators (operational management entities), and communication infrastructure (data exchange enablers).

\begin{definitions}[\textbf{Cybersecurity in an IoT Ecosystem}]
\textit{Intrinsic properties of the design, development, structure, integration, operation, and physical conditions that determine the vulnerability, robustness, resilience, and response to disturbances or malicious interventions in IoT ecosystems.}
\end{definitions}

We likewise adopt the reconceptualization of the Root of Trust (RoT) as a multi-phase \emph{process}, rather than a static hardware component, established in \citet{campos2026identity}: entities establish, exchange, and securely store identity parameters within a trusted environment before any operational communication begins. This process accommodates heterogeneous device capabilities---from constrained microcontrollers to cloud servers---supports both symmetric and asymmetric cryptographic schemes, and establishes the trusted context required for provisioning, authentication, and secure session establishment across all entity types. When Phase~1 (parameter generation) is instantiated through a Physically Unclonable Function (PUF) rather than random generation followed by persistent storage, Phase~2 (secure storage) is correspondingly trivialized: the entity's private identity parameter is never stored at rest---only non-secret helper data required to reconstruct it deterministically---removing the secure-storage attack surface rather than merely hardening it. Stage~6.1.3 (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) instantiates this process concretely for the case study's entity types.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
% ============================================================

The methodology is organized into four phases and seven stages (Figure~\ref{fig:metodologia}). Each stage produces documented outputs that serve as inputs to the subsequent stage, and is itself decomposed into numbered sub-processes (e.g., 2.2.4, 6.1.3) that the case-study evidence cites throughout this article; the Supplementary Material gives both the generic, technology-agnostic specification of each process (Tables~S1--S4) and its instantiation for the SMG case study (Tables~S5--S8).

\subsection{Phase A: Organizational (Stage~1)}

This phase addresses organizational considerations for IoT adoption. Stage~1 establishes the problem to be solved, operating policies, development policies, and cybersecurity criteria. The key processes are: problem definition with cost-benefit analysis (1.1); operating policies covering functional objectives, financial constraints, and regulatory compliance (1.2); development policies defining an iterative strategy using the TRL framework, timelines, and resource allocation (1.3); and cybersecurity policies covering criticality assessment, technological sovereignty, data sovereignty, supply chain security, and applicable government guidelines including Secure-by-Design \citep{ukgov2024sbdprinciples} and Privacy-by-Design \citep{enisa2017baseline} principles (1.4). Processes 1.4.2--1.4.3 operationalize the organizational-strategy layer of the sovereignty framework established in \citet{campos2026identity}, under which technological and data sovereignty are treated as emergent properties of who controls the entities' Root of Trust process rather than as external policy overlays.

\subsection{Phase B: Architecture (Stages~2--3)}

Stage~2 defines the IoT ecosystem architecture at the entity level: integration diagram, communication topology, entity hierarchy, functional processes, data flow, and scalability strategy. Critically, Stage~2 instantiates the formal identity model established in \citet{campos2026identity}, under which each entity $X$ in ecosystem $E$ possesses an identity:
\begin{equation}
    I^E_X = P^E_{X\;pub} \cup P^E_{X\;priv}
\end{equation}
where public parameters $P^E_{X\;pub}$ serve as identifiers and private parameters $P^E_{X\;priv}$ enable cryptographic operations, and authentication parameters $P^E_{X\;auth} \subset I^E_X$ and shared secrets $P^E_{X\;SS} \subset P^E_{X\;auth}$ are derived from this identity foundation (Figure~\ref{fig:identidad}). This methodology extends that model with an explicit cardinality count for each entity type $X$: the public-parameter and private-parameter counts are:
\begin{equation}
    n^E_X = |P^E_{X\;pub}|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    k^E_X = |P^E_{X\;priv}|
\end{equation}
so that the total identity parameter count is $n^E_X + k^E_X$ and the table columns report the two component counts and their sum.

Shared secrets $P_{SS}$ enable cryptographic operations; authentication parameters $P_{auth}$ prove identity; private parameters $P_{priv}$ are never disclosed; public parameters $P_{pub}$ serve as identifiers. In the SMG, the node's private identity parameter (its PUF response) lives in $P_{priv}$ but is never persisted to storage---only the non-secret mask/ECC helper data used to reconstruct it lives in ESP32~NVS, as $P_{pub}$.}

Stage~3 specifies the services required for entity operation: secure storage subsystems, access control mechanisms, identity parameter storage strategies, and regulatory compliance integration. For constrained devices, Stage~3 requires an explicit cost/risk evaluation of persistent secure-storage options (hardware security modules, encrypted flash partitions) against physically-derived alternatives that avoid persisting a secret altogether; a Physically Unclonable Function (PUF) that regenerates the private identity parameter on demand from non-secret helper data is one such alternative, and its selection at Stage~4 is a direct consequence of this Stage~3 evaluation rather than an independent design choice. Servers and users retain conventional secrets management and MFA as generic Stage-3 options; the SMG case study itself implements only a single-factor password hash for users/admins, not MFA (Table~\ref{tab:identity}, Limitations).

\subsection{Phase C: Implementation (Stages~4--5)}

Stage~4 selects enabling technologies aligned with policies from Stage~1 and architecture from Stages~2--3. Technology choices are evaluated against maturity level (TRL), vulnerability landscape, and supply chain risk. For the SMG, Stage~4 selects \texttt{esp32\_puflib} \citep{stanicek2022esp32puflib}, an SRAM-based PUF implementation for the ESP32 family, as the identity-generation mechanism motivated by the Stage~3 secure-storage evaluation above. Because \texttt{esp32\_puflib} is implemented as a native C/ESP-IDF component, Stage~4 also selects a C/ESP-IDF firmware architecture for the node, so that the PUF library is linked directly without a language-runtime wrapper; the same decision selects ESP-IDF's native \texttt{mbedtls}/\texttt{esp\_aes} for AES-256-GCM and ECDSA support. This platform choice is itself a direct, traceable consequence of the Stage~3 evaluation, not an independent implementation preference. Stage~5 implements all ecosystem elements: servers, devices, databases, communication infrastructure, IoT services, and applications. Security is enforced through secure coding practices, unit testing of security mechanisms, and validation against applicable security guidelines (Secure-by-Design principles \citep{ukgov2024sbdprinciples}, NIST IoT Cybersecurity Guidance \citep{NIST_IoT_2020}, and OWASP IoT Top~10).

\subsection{Phase D: Operation (Stages~6--7)}

Stage~6 covers deployment, configuration, and testing: individual component validation, infrastructure deployment, \emph{Root of Trust execution} for all entities (Process~6.1.3), general operation verification, and security testing (penetration testing at higher TRLs; tamper verification at TRL~3). Stage~7 addresses ongoing operation: monitoring, element maintenance, information lifecycle management, threat intelligence, element updates, incident response, and network infrastructure oversight.

\subsection{Iterative Development and Maintenance}

The methodology supports iterative advancement through TRL-guided cycles (ITER) and three structured maintenance points: M1 (during implementation, triggers technology re-evaluation), M2 (during deployment, triggers code revision), and M3 (during operation, triggers architectural redesign). M1 fires when an implementation-phase review reveals that the chosen technology cannot satisfy a stage requirement; M2 fires when deployment-phase testing reveals that the implementation requires code-level revision to meet a security or functional requirement; M3 fires when operation-phase monitoring reveals that the deployed architecture cannot accommodate an observed operational pattern. This structure supports progressive maturation from proof-of-concept to production deployment.

\subsection{Root of Trust Establishment}

Building on the four-phase Root of Trust process of \citet{campos2026identity}, we instantiate its parameter generation, secure storage, exchange, and peer-management phases as concrete protocol message sequences for both cryptographic schemes.

\textbf{PUF-seeded device identity (resource-constrained devices):} Rather than generating a random device key pair and persisting the private key in flash, the device's identity key pair is derived on demand from a Physically Unclonable Function (PUF), instantiated via \texttt{esp32\_puflib} \citep{stanicek2022esp32puflib}. \emph{Enrollment} (performed once, Table~\ref{tab:rotpsim}) reads the ESP32's RTC~FAST~SRAM power-up state across repeated measurements and derives two artifacts---a stable-bit mask and error-correction (helper) data---that are stored on the device and allow the same physical response to be reconstructed, with correction, on every subsequent boot. Reconstructing the response from these artifacts alone, without the chip, is intended to be infeasible, consistent with the design goal of SRAM-based PUFs \citep{guajardo2007fpga}; however, repetition-code helper data is known to leak some response information \citep{dodis2004fuzzy, maes2013physically, delvaux2015helper}, so this is a design property, not a proven guarantee for this construction (see Limitations). On every boot, the reconstructed response deterministically derives an elliptic-curve (P-256) key pair via HKDF-based key derivation (RFC~5869) followed by rejection sampling to a uniform scalar; the device registers its public key with the server once, at enrollment, and the corresponding private key is never transmitted, stored, or otherwise disclosed---it exists only transiently in RAM for the duration of a single boot. \emph{Session identification} (Table~\ref{tab:rotpsim_b}) is asymmetric on the device side and symmetric on the server side: the device signs a server-issued nonce with its private key (ECDSA), verified against its registered public key; the server proves possession of its own pinned, per-node verifier by hashing it with a device-issued nonce, checked against the value pinned at enrollment. Each side also exchanges a fresh ephemeral public key, bound into the same proofs; the resulting ECDH secret, not the long-term verifier alone, anchors the session key (Table~\ref{tab:rotpsim_b}), and neither proof nor ephemeral key is reused across sessions. The device's NVS holds only the non-secret stable-bit mask/ECC data ($P_{pub}$) and the pinned per-node server verifier $V_B$; its own private key ($sk_A$, derived fresh from $\text{PUF}_A$ each boot) and its ephemeral key pair exist only transiently in RAM (Section~\ref{sec:conceptual}).

\textbf{Server identity:} the server is not a good physical candidate for PUF-based identity (no comparable unclonable substrate is assumed for the RPi4), so its identity parameter is a conventionally-provisioned verifier $V_B$, generated fresh per enrolled device and pinned on that device as its server-authentication trust anchor. $V_B$ is deliberately not shared across the fleet: recovering one device's pinned $V_B$ lets an attacker impersonate the server only to that single device, and (Table~\ref{tab:rotpsim_b}; Limitations) no longer by itself discloses that device's past or future P3 traffic. Server authentication is symmetric: possession of $V_B$ is exactly what the server must prove per session, via the same nonce-bound hash structure that gives the device's proof its freshness guarantee; its confidentiality on the device side is bounded by physical security (Information Disclosure, Table~\ref{tab:stride_b}; Limitations). Device authentication, by contrast, is asymmetric: the server stores only each device's public key, so a full server compromise discloses no value sufficient to impersonate any device (Section~\ref{subsec:strideprot}). The two entities' parameters are thus deliberately asymmetric, bounding the single point of failure a fully-symmetric scheme would place on the server.

\textbf{Asymmetric scheme (capable devices):} Entities generate public/private key pairs, exchange public keys with identifiers, and store the peer's public key for subsequent authentication using ISO/IEC~9798-3-style signature-based mutual authentication \citep{isoiec9798} (Table~\ref{tab:rotpasym}).

The entity-specific separation of identity parameters produced a direct, unambiguous mapping to implementation artifacts: \texttt{mask}$_A$ and \texttt{ecc}$_A$ correspond to distinct NVS keys written at enrollment time; no parameter redefinition was required at the specification level for those two. \texttt{id\_a} now follows the same path when provisioned: it defaults to a compile-time placeholder shared by every unit, but a per-device NVS value overrides it when provisioned (Table~\ref{tab:identity}, note~$^\S$; see Limitations for the fix history). In the present case study, $P_{auth}$ and $P_{priv}$ coincide for every entity type (all private parameters participate in authentication, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:identity}); this is a case-study-specific coincidence, not a general property of the model---\citet{campos2026identity} define only the broader relation $P_{auth} \subset I$ (Figure~\ref{fig:identidad}), so future case studies may include a private parameter not used for authentication (e.g., a long-term audit key), in which case $P_{auth} \subsetneq P_{priv}$ rather than $P_{auth} = P_{priv}$.

$^\dagger$\textbf{Note on the PUF response as a non-persistent private parameter:} the formal identity model $I^E_X = P^E_{X\;pub} \cup P^E_{X\;priv}$ (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) classifies a parameter as private or public by its confidentiality role, not by whether it is stored. The IoT Node's PUF response satisfies $P_{priv}$'s role---it is never disclosed and underlies the device's authentication proof---while never existing as data at rest: it is regenerated from \texttt{mask}$_A$/\texttt{ecc}$_A$ (both $P_{pub}$) on every boot. This is the concrete instance of the Phase~2 (secure storage) trivialization noted in Section~\ref{sec:conceptual}.

$^\ddagger$\textbf{Note on $V_B$ as the server's private/authentication parameter:} the server's row describes its own proof of identity to a device, made by demonstrating knowledge of $V_B$ (Table~\ref{tab:rotpsim_b}) --- the server-side analogue of the Node's PUF response. \texttt{id\_b} (fleet-wide, non-secret) sits under $P_{pub}$ with \texttt{url/ip} and \texttt{port}. The device separately pins its own copy of $V_B$ as a trust anchor (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, ``Server identity''); this is a stored reference to \emph{another} entity's parameter, not an additional Node parameter, so it is not double-counted.

$^\S$\textbf{Note on \texttt{id\_a}:} defaults to a compile-time placeholder shared by every unit, overridden by a per-device NVS value when provisioned (\texttt{tools/provision\_server\_ref.py --id-a}); it identifies the device on the wire and, as a public value, is bound into the signed P2 transcript (Table~\ref{tab:rotpsim_b}) alongside the nonces and ephemeral keys, but it is not itself a secret or the cryptographic primitive the proof rests on -- that is the PUF-derived key pair (see Limitations for the fix history).

\subsection{Security Mechanism Analysis: STRIDE--to--Protocol Mapping}
\label{subsec:strideprot}

The methodology-level STRIDE analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:stride}) identifies mechanism categories that address each threat category at the framework level, independent of implementation technology. This subsection demonstrates how the SMG's Stage~4 technology selection instantiates those mechanisms through three authentication and data-exchange protocol phases (P1--P3), establishing a complete traceability chain from threat identification to deployed countermeasure.

\textbf{Scope note:} Phases P1--P3 constitute an \emph{implementation-specific} Stage~4 technology choice for the SMG (see Introduction scope note). Any protocol providing equivalent mechanism categories---pre-deployment identity isolation (P1), nonce-bound mutual identification without key transmission (P2), and per-message authenticated encryption (P3)---would satisfy the same STRIDE and OWASP properties against the same methodology requirements. This design intentionally omits a cryptographic session-chain/audit mechanism; Tables~\ref{tab:stride_a}/\ref{tab:stride_b} and the Limitations (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) record the resulting Repudiation/I8 trade-off explicitly.

\noindent\textbf{Spoofing} is countered at two layers: P1 ties valid identity to physical access to the specific enrolled chip rather than a copyable stored secret; P2's nonce-bound challenge requires fresh, per-session proof from each side---an ECDSA signature from the device, a hash-reconstruction proof over $V_B$ from the server---without revealing the physical response, $sk_A$, or $V_B$ itself, confirmed bidirectionally before any session key is derived.

\noindent\textbf{Tampering} is detected at message creation time by the AES-256-GCM authentication tag on every message (P3); P2's mutual identification additionally binds the session key to both parties' proven identities. The scheme has no session-level hash chain, so retrospective re-verification of message ordering is unavailable (downgraded to \pyes\ in Table~\ref{tab:stridecoverage}; see Limitations).

\noindent\textbf{Repudiation} coverage is evidentiary rather than cryptographic: P3 messages are authenticated in transit and the server logs each session (\texttt{node\_id}, timestamp, Stage~7.1), but no independently-verifiable chain binds messages to a session without trusting the server's logs, a deliberate trade-off for a smaller on-device footprint (see Limitations).

\noindent\textbf{Information Disclosure} is addressed at the source (P1: no key ever exists in storage, only non-secret helper data), during derivation (P2: the physical response is reconstructed transiently in RAM and never transmitted; neither $sk_A$ nor $V_B$ is ever sent, only proofs over them), and in transit (P3: per-message AES-256-GCM under a key that also depends on a discarded per-session ECDH secret, with random nonces).

\noindent\textbf{Denial of Service} is partially addressed at P2: the server's first reply ($R_B$) costs one nonce, one ephemeral P-256 key-pair generation, and one SHA-256 hash --- modest, but incurred for any requester presenting a registered \texttt{id\_a}, which defaults to a shared, publicly visible placeholder unless a node has been given a distinct per-device value (Limitations) --- yet no further session state is committed---and no \texttt{session\_ack} issued---until the device's ECDSA proof $R_A$ verifies, imposing an asymmetric cost on an attacker holding a session open. Volumetric and application-layer flooding beyond the identification gate remain outside P1--P3's scope; Stage~7 trust-based isolation (Table~\ref{tab:stride_b}) is the mandated mechanism, implemented in the SMG's original server modules but not yet ported to the current RoT server (see Limitations).

\noindent\textbf{Elevation of Privilege} is prevented at P1, since entity type is determined by which identification channel a party uses, not a field inside a shared message: nodes authenticate exclusively through the P1--P3 RoT protocol, while users and administrators use a separate, credential-based API (Table~\ref{tab:identity}); no message in Tables~\ref{tab:rotpsim}/\ref{tab:rotpsim_b} carries an entity-type field. P3 API access is bound to the P2 session key, so a node session cannot invoke user- or admin-scoped endpoints.

Four findings stand out. First and second, two defects were found and fixed during this validation, not pre-existing design properties: a local reliability gate miscalibrated to reject every reconstruction outright (an earlier round under that gate, \texttt{bench\_results\_20261015/}, returned 0/30; this fix did not change the derived key pair), and a helper-data-manipulation oracle in the key derivation whose first mitigation attempt left one of two helper-data pairs unbound (this fix, in both of its passes, does change the derived key pair, requiring re-enrollment each time); both are detailed, with their discovery and fix history, in Limitations. The round reported here (\texttt{bench\_results\_20261016\_hdm2/}) reflects both fixes fully applied, after two earlier, now-superseded rounds captured the intermediate states (dataset pointers below). That the second fix's retry-path binding is genuinely load-bearing, not merely read without effect, was confirmed directly: a single bit flipped in either retry-path helper-data blob (leaving the other three blobs and the physical PUF untouched) caused the server to reject the signature, while first-attempt reconstruction --- which never reads those blobs --- kept reporting a normal error rate; restoring the unmodified image immediately re-established a successful handshake. Third, the end-to-end protocol---PUF reconstruction, nonce-bound mutual identification in both directions, and AES-256-GCM data exchange---succeeded in every trial, confirming a stable identity (and key pair) despite per-attempt measurement noise that now only reaches the server's cryptographic check. Fourth, the handshake latency splits into three phases, detailed in Table~\ref{tab:pufbench}: device-side PUF reconstruction and key-pair derivation followed by server authentication, the device's ECDSA sign/verify, and ECDH key derivation; total latency is tightly clustered (median 1300\,ms, mean $1313.3\pm47.6$\,ms) but about 50\,ms slower by mean than the first-generation (two-blob) HDM round (median 1250\,ms, mean $1262.7\pm47.9$\,ms) and about 245\,ms slower by mean than the pre-mitigation round (median 1060\,ms, mean $1067.7\pm23.6$\,ms); the first phase rose by 44\,ms relative to the first-generation HDM round (Table~\ref{tab:pufbench}), consistent with, though not proof of, the additional NVS reads and hashing the extended binding performs during key-pair derivation, before the first message is sent. A separate, later comparison under a different firmware build (Limitations, per-device \texttt{id\_a} fix) found a phase-1 difference an order of magnitude larger than this one with no corresponding code change in that phase, so this attribution should be read as plausible, not established. The server log for this round shows 33 ``handshake OK'' entries (30 reported trials plus 3 incidental verification/restore handshakes) and 5 ``FAILED'' entries (2 single-bit-flip tamper trials plus 3 FT-06 trials below). The 30 trials are repeated measures on one device/enrollment, not independent samples (Limitations). Raw logs and summaries for all six bench rounds are in the companion artifact repository under \texttt{case study SMG/\allowbreak Implementation/\allowbreak smg\_puf\_rot\_node/} (Materials Availability): \texttt{bench\_results\_20261016\_hdm2/} is the round reported here; \texttt{bench\_results\_20261015\_hdm/} (30/30, first-generation two-blob HDM binding; mean $1262.7\pm47.9$\,ms), \texttt{bench\_results\_20261015\_fixed/} (30/30, same relaxed error-rate gate but the pre-HDM-mitigation identity; mean $1067.7\pm23.6$\,ms), \texttt{bench\_results\_20261015/} (0/30, the miscalibrated-gate round above), \texttt{bench\_results\_20261013/} (30/30, forward-secrecy added but the retry path's error-rate gate not yet enforced; mean $1131\pm50$\,ms), and \texttt{bench\_results\_20261005/} (pre-forward-secrecy protocol; mean $558.0\pm181.2$\,ms) are all superseded and kept for provenance. All reported standard deviations in this paper are population (not sample) statistics.

\textbf{Answer to CSQ3:} The RoT process established verifiable mutual authentication for SMG\_NODE\_01 across $N=30$ successive hard-reset trials (30/30 end-to-end success, Table~\ref{tab:pufbench}): the device authenticated the server via a nonce-bound hash-reconstruction proof over a per-node verifier, and the server authenticated the device via an ECDSA signature verified against the device's registered public key, with neither the device's private key, the server's per-node verifier, nor the ECDH shared secret ever transmitted. Median handshake latency was 1300\,ms (mean $1313.3 \pm 47.6$\,ms), splitting across device-side PUF reconstruction and key-pair derivation plus server authentication (680.0\,ms), the device's ECDSA sign/verify (359.7\,ms), and ECDH key derivation (273.7\,ms, Table~\ref{tab:pufbench}). The forward-secrecy fix added the ephemeral key-pair generation (first phase) and the ECDH computation (third phase, which did not exist in the original protocol); the helper-data-manipulation fix added its cost to the first phase, where the key pair is derived (Limitations). SMG\_NODE\_02 was separately registered and validated under this scheme (own $N=30$ trial, 30/30, Limitations), confirming the reliability profile is not specific to one physical chip.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

This work addresses two barriers to secure IoT ecosystem development: the security activation gap at the operational level, and the absence of a structured development methodology that integrates cybersecurity from the earliest organizational stage. The results of the confirmatory case study and expert validation are discussed below in relation to each research question.

\textbf{CSQ1 --- Methodology guidance:} The stage-by-stage traceability (Tables~\ref{tab:traceability_a} and~\ref{tab:traceability_b}) demonstrates that the methodology produces actionable decision points at every stage. The organizational phase (Stage~1) carries the highest leverage: classifying the SMG as critical infrastructure (Process~1.4.1) directly constrained the authentication scheme (requiring mutual cryptographic authentication), the storage approach (requiring isolation of identity material from firmware, met at TRL~3 by the NVS-partition/PUF design; a dedicated secure element is an ITER~2 requirement, see Limitations), and the access control granularity (requiring node, user, and admin distinction). Absent an explicit organizational stage, these constraints might not have been systematically propagated to architectural and implementation decisions.

\textbf{CSQ2 --- Identity model:} The formal separation of identity into $P_{pub}$, $P_{priv}$, and $P_{auth}$ subsets yielded a technology-agnostic security specification that translated directly to ESP32 NVS storage for the PUF helper data and to an HKDF-derived ECDSA key pair for the private, non-persistent PUF response; \texttt{id\_a}'s translation now also follows this path when provisioned (overriding the default compile-time placeholder with a per-device NVS value, see Limitations). The 14-parameter model produced zero ambiguity across 4 entity types, confirming the model's expressiveness for heterogeneous IoT ecosystems, including resource-constrained devices.

\textbf{CSQ3 --- Root of Trust verifiability:} The $N=30$ real hard-reset trial (Section~\ref{subsec:pufbench}) establishes that the handshake, including the forward-secrecy and helper-data-manipulation fixes, authenticates reliably (30/30, every trial on the first attempt) despite per-attempt PUF measurement noise, with the P3 demo message delivered and decrypted successfully every time -- not merely that it can succeed once.

\textbf{CSQ4 --- Threat coverage:} The unweighted OWASP IoT Top~10 coverage (6/10 full; 4/10 partial: I4 secure-update policy only, I7 data encrypted in transit but not at rest pending ITER~2 flash encryption, I8 device management without a verifiable chain, I10 physical hardening) indicates the methodology systematically addresses known IoT threat categories. The STRIDE analysis operates at two levels: the methodology-level mapping (Tables~\ref{tab:stride_a}/\ref{tab:stride_b}, Section~\ref{subsec:stride}) shows every threat addressed by a mechanism mandated by at least one stage; the protocol-level mapping (Tables~\ref{tab:phases_a}--\ref{tab:stridecoverage}, Section~\ref{subsec:strideprot}) traces each mechanism to its implementing phase. All six categories are addressed, four by multiple independent phases. The three partial OWASP items reflect two deliberate trade-offs (I4, I8---see Limitations) and one inherent scope boundary (I10); the coverage figure is unweighted, not severity-weighted.

\textbf{Maintenance cycles:} At least seven maintenance moments were triggered during Stages~5--6 (M2 firings for code-level revision, Section~\ref{subsec:pufbench}, Limitations), confirming the methodology's M2 pathway operates as specified at TRL~3. Each was caught during deployment-phase bench testing or subsequent review, before or alongside the reported trial; the case study does not establish a counterfactual about whether any would have surfaced only later absent that structure, since bench testing of this kind is standard practice regardless. M1 and M3 were not triggered and remain empirically unvalidated, available for future maintenance points and the operation-phase TRL transition.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

\begin{sloppypar}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Single topology:} Only centralized topology was implemented at TRL~3. Decentralized and mesh topologies supported by Stage~2.1.4 remain to be validated empirically.
    \item \textbf{TRL~3 scope:} The proof of concept operates in a controlled laboratory environment. Environmental variation, scalability beyond 2 nodes, and production-grade hardening (JTAG disable, secure boot) are deferred to ITER~2.
    \item \textbf{Primary-agent power dependency:} In the TRL~3 bench configuration the RPi4 primary agent is powered from the same DC bus it coordinates (via a DC/AC inverter); SSR duty transients and node resets cause primary-agent brownouts that can terminate sustained bench trials early. This coupling constrains sustained concurrent operation and is an artifact of the laboratory power arrangement rather than the methodology; ITER~2 will use an independent primary-agent supply.
    \item \textbf{Forward secrecy (ephemeral ECDH added, hardware-validated):} An earlier revision derived $Key_{ab}$ from $V_B$ and the handshake nonces alone, so recovering $V_B$ (e.g., from a flash dump, Table~\ref{tab:stride_b}) together with a recorded handshake sufficed to recompute that session's key. $Key_{ab}$ now additionally depends on $Z$, a per-session ECDH secret discarded immediately after use, closing that gap: recovering $V_B$ alone no longer yields $Key_{ab}$ for any session. The fix is implemented (\texttt{rot\_session.c}/\allowbreak\texttt{rot\_challenge\_\allowbreak service.py}), passes the host-side protocol test suite (including a dedicated test that two handshakes against the same node yield different keys), and was validated end-to-end on the physical ESP32 against the real RPi4 server with a refreshed $N=30$ bench round (30/30 success, Table~\ref{tab:pufbench}, superseding the earlier pre-fix timing retained in the artifact repository for provenance).
    \item \textbf{Device-side PUF entropy:} The server stores only $pk_A$ and $V_B$ (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), neither of which recreates $sk_A$ if disclosed; however, $sk_A$'s security still depends on the actual (not merely designed) entropy of the PUF response it derives from. Independent enrollments on one SMG node have measured 421--422-byte ($\sim$3368--3376-bit) stable responses, somewhat above the library's $\sim$380--400-byte reference-hardware estimate (chip-to-chip and enrollment-to-enrollment variation, discussed below). Raw length is not min-entropy: repetition-code helper data is known to leak some response information \citep{dodis2004fuzzy, maes2013physically, delvaux2015helper}, so 3368 raw bits do not imply 256 bits of adversarial min-entropy. A dedicated min-entropy assessment (e.g., NIST SP 800-90B) is the direct way to confirm sufficient min-entropy for the 256-bit scalar it seeds; this is a non-trivial statistical analysis of repeated-boot raw measurement data that this TRL~3 validation does not yet include, and is deferred to ITER~2 alongside the other hardening items in this list.
    \item \textbf{Two PUF/NVS defects found and fixed during this work (full discovery-and-fix history in Supplementary Material~S2):} \texttt{esp32\_puflib}'s enrollment procedure left a raw per-bit measurement blob (\texttt{PUF\_FREQUENCY}) unerased in NVS, sufficient to reconstruct the PUF reference without the physical device, contrary to this paper's claim that no raw response is ever written to flash; the fix zero-overwrites and erases it post-enrollment, though NVS's log-structured storage means an earlier, un-zeroed version can still be byte-recoverable until garbage collection runs (SMG\_NODE\_01's deployed NVS carries none of this residue, as a side effect of how its server reference was provisioned, not a property the library guarantees in general). Separately, the public helper-data blobs (\texttt{PUF\_MASK}/\texttt{ECC\_DATA} and their deep-sleep-retry counterparts) were stored without integrity protection, letting an attacker with flash-write access use the ECC decoder's error count as an oracle on individual response bits; the fix binds SHA-256 of all four blobs into the HKDF key derivation (applied in two passes, after a second review found the first covered only two of the four blobs), confirmed load-bearing by a direct single-bit-flip test. Only flash encryption and secure boot (ITER~2 candidates, not yet enabled) close these fully. Only the second fix (the helper-data binding) changes the derived key pair and required re-enrolling SMG\_NODE\_01, in each of its two passes; the \texttt{PUF\_FREQUENCY} erasure does not change the derived key (re-validation results below).
    \item \textbf{PUF library build and bench status (validated on both nodes):} \texttt{esp32\_puflib} \citep{stanicek2022esp32puflib} is a native C/ESP-IDF component, linked directly into the node's firmware without a language-runtime wrapper; it and its RPi4 counterpart build cleanly and have been bench-validated end-to-end on both of the SMG's physical ESP32 units ($N=30$ each, 30/30, Section~\ref{subsec:pufbench}). Physical distinctness across chips was additionally confirmed by cloning SMG\_NODE\_01's NVS onto SMG\_NODE\_02's hardware: rejected by the server in all 11 cross-chip attempts across four successive firmware generations (Supplementary Material~S2, Incident~4), since detection rests entirely on the server's ECDSA verification, not any device-side check -- relaxing the device-side diagnostic only changes when, not whether, a wrong reconstruction is caught.
    \item \textbf{Sensor/EMS modules not yet implemented (C/ESP-IDF firmware scope):} The \texttt{smg\_puf\_rot\_node} C/ESP-IDF firmware currently implements only the identity (PUF), Root of Trust, and secure-communication layers; fuzzy Energy Management System and sensor-acquisition logic have not yet been implemented in this firmware. A full end-to-end SMG deployment requires this additional work, distinct from and in addition to the hardware bench validation above. The same gap applies to the server side: the primary agent's anomaly-based trust scoring and quarantine logic (Stage~7.2.1) exists in \texttt{smg\_primary\_server}'s original module set (\texttt{auth\_service.py}, \texttt{ems\_coordinator.py}) but has not yet been ported to \texttt{rot\_challenge\_service.py}, the server counterpart of the current RoT protocol; DoS/I3 coverage claims elsewhere in this paper that cite trust scoring describe the methodology-mandated mechanism and its prior instantiation, not a property of the currently-deployed RoT server.
    \item \textbf{No cryptographic session-chain audit (deliberate design simplification):} The SMG's Root of Trust does not implement a per-message hash chain to provide a retrospectively-verifiable, third-party-auditable record of session integrity. Message-level integrity is provided by the AES-256-GCM authentication tag (P3), but there is no cryptographic chain binding messages within a session, and Repudiation/I8 (device management) coverage is therefore evidentiary (server-side session logs) rather than independently verifiable (Table~\ref{tab:stride_a}, Table~\ref{tab:owasp_b}). Introducing a lightweight, chain-free sequencing mechanism (e.g., a monotonic per-session counter bound into the GCM associated data) without the overhead of a full chain audit is a candidate ITER~2 refinement if reviewers or subsequent evaluation identify this trade-off as unacceptable.
    \item \textbf{PUF reliability on the deployed hardware (cross-node data now available; thermal/voltage characterization pending):} \texttt{esp32\_puflib} targets a design bit-flip probability of 0.001--0.005 via a stable-bit mask and 8$\times$-repetition-code error correction, yielding an estimated $\sim$380--400-byte stable response in the library's own design notes; independent enrollments on SMG\_NODE\_01 have produced 421--422\,bytes, consistently reproducible but somewhat above that estimate (a plausible chip-to-chip variation, not a conformance failure, since the library documents its figure as an estimate). Reconstruction reliability was measured over six $N=30$ bench rounds on SMG\_NODE\_01 across the project's history (Table~\ref{tab:pufbench}), including one under a since-relaxed reliability gate that rejected every trial (Supplementary Material~S2, Incident~3); the round reported there shows no bimodal attempt pattern, with every trial succeeding on the first attempt -- the two-attempt pattern seen in the 2026-10-13 round is now understood as an artifact of that gate, not a genuine circuit-level difference between the library's two reset depths. That round's attempt-1 error rate rose measurably across its own $\sim$5-minute duration, suggesting within-run thermal drift; SMG\_NODE\_01's canonical round, though shorter ($\sim$3 minutes), shows the same upward trend (0.342\% over its first ten trials versus 0.436\% over its last ten; Spearman $\rho=0.74$), without affecting first-attempt success. SMG\_NODE\_02, registered and bench-validated end-to-end once a per-device \texttt{id\_a} provisioning gap was closed (above), produced a 420-byte stable response (two bytes shorter than SMG\_NODE\_01's 422) and comparable reliability under the current firmware build: $N=30$, 30/30 success, every trial on the first attempt, attempt-1 raw error 0.403--0.531\% (median 0.455\%, modestly higher than SMG\_NODE\_01's canonical-round range of 0.308--0.485\%, mean 0.397\%) (raw logs and summary in the artifact repository at \texttt{case\_study\_SMG/\allowbreak Implementation/\allowbreak smg\_puf\_rot\_node/\allowbreak bench\_results\_20261019\_node02/}, Materials Availability). This confirms the reliability profile is not an artifact specific to one chip. Handshake timing is not reported here as a cross-chip comparison: SMG\_NODE\_02's firmware build was several days newer than SMG\_NODE\_01's canonical round, and a same-build regression check on SMG\_NODE\_01 itself (6 handshakes observed, of which only 1 raw log was archived) suggests build-to-build timing varies by roughly 250\,ms independent of any code change relevant to the handshake path -- large enough that neither a cross-build nor a same-build comparison at this evidence level supports a firm latency claim; identifying the cause is deferred to future work. Two items remain unmeasured: (a) sensitivity to temperature/supply-voltage variation in a controlled way, deferred to ITER~2; and (b) SMG\_NODE\_02's own thermal-drift trend over a longer run, not separately characterized here.
    \item \textbf{Single application domain:} Energy management is the only domain tested. The expert questionnaire (N=14, Section~\ref{subsec:expertval}) supports the technology-agnostic design intent (B13 mean 3.79/5) but also recorded two dissenting scores on this same item (2/5 and 1/5), with the dissenting evaluators characterizing cross-domain generalizability as an unvalidated hypothesis; empirical validation in additional domains remains future work.
    \item \textbf{Physical hardening:} I10 (physical hardening) and hardware-based Root of Trust (e.g., ATECC608 secure element) are not addressed at TRL~3 but are specified as ITER~2 requirements.
    \item \textbf{Identity lifecycle (methodology gap):} The methodology as presented does not specify a complete identity lifecycle process (parameter rotation, revocation, recovery after compromise without full re-provisioning, ownership transfer, and decommissioning). This is a gap in the methodology itself---not solely a TRL~3 scope limitation---since recovery from a compromised node currently requires re-running the full Root of Trust. An expert evaluator (Section~\ref{subsec:expertval}) identified this as the single addition with the greatest potential impact on practical applicability; it is the highest-priority extension for the next methodology revision and is deferred to ITER~2.
    \item \textbf{User/administrator password hashing:} Users and administrators authenticate via a plain SHA-256 hash of their password (Table~\ref{tab:identity}), not a salted, slow key-derivation function (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2) --- weaker than current practice, and a candidate ITER~2 hardening item; it does not affect the PUF-based IoT node scheme, which uses no password.
    \item \textbf{WiFi credential provisioning (fixed; fleet-sharing residual):} An earlier firmware revision compiled the WiFi SSID/password into source as a fixed constant, identical for every build and visible in the published firmware source. The credential is now provisioned into NVS out-of-band by \texttt{tools/provision\_server\_ref.py} (the same mechanism already used for $V_B$), so no WiFi credential value appears in firmware source or the compiled binary. One property is unchanged by this fix and is not a firmware defect: every node on the fleet's WPA2-PSK access point necessarily shares the same passphrase, so recovering one node's NVS still discloses the credential needed to join that network, same as recovering any WPA2-PSK passphrase would. Rotating it still requires re-provisioning every node's NVS image, not merely one.
    \item \textbf{Per-device node identifier (fixed; found while registering a second node):} \texttt{id\_a}, the device's wire identifier and server-registry key, was a compile-time placeholder shared by every unit built from the same firmware source, which blocked registering a second physical node under this scheme at all: the server registry is keyed on \texttt{id\_a}, so a second node with the same value would have overwritten the first's registry entry. \texttt{rot\_identity\_get\_id\_a()} now reads \texttt{rot\_identity/id\_a} from NVS if present, falling back to the compile-time placeholder otherwise, so each node can be provisioned with a distinct value (\texttt{tools/provision\_server\_ref.py --id-a}) without a firmware change; \texttt{id\_a} is a public value bound into the signed P2 transcript (\texttt{rot\_session.c}), not the secret the proof rests on (the PUF-derived key pair), so this fix does not change what is cryptographically proved, only registry addressing. The fallback was verified not to disturb SMG\_NODE\_01 (6/6 handshakes against its existing registry entry, byte-identical before and after the firmware update); SMG\_NODE\_02 was then provisioned with a distinct \texttt{id\_a} and validated end-to-end (Section~\ref{subsec:pufbench}).
    \item \textbf{Expert validation evaluated an earlier instantiation, not the final PUF-based system (scope of what the panel's ratings establish):} The reference document distributed to evaluators (June~24, 2026) does not merely use more conservative figures than the final implementation; it describes a materially different instantiation of the same methodology: three ESP32 nodes (not two), MicroPython firmware (not C/ESP-IDF), a random per-node key plus HKDF-SHA256 shared secret for RoT execution (not a PUF-derived identity), a continuous hash-chain integrity mechanism (not present in the current protocol), and a 12-parameter identity model (not 14). Four questionnaire items name these specifics directly (B6, B8, B9, B10) and are excluded from the headline mean for that reason (Section~\ref{subsec:expertval}); the retained 10 items ask about the methodology's process-level properties -- stage structure, artifact verifiability, the public/private/authentication identity taxonomy, technology-agnostic STRIDE mapping, cross-domain applicability -- which both instantiations share, since they concern the methodology prescribing a step, not which cryptographic primitive a given case study chose to satisfy it. The panel's quantitative ratings should accordingly be read as evaluating the methodology's conceptual soundness and process structure, as demonstrated through a representative (if since-superseded) instantiation; they are not a validation of the final PUF-based implementation's specific technical choices, which the $N=30$ hardware bench and functional tests (Section~\ref{subsec:pufbench}) validate separately and are not contingent on the panel's scope. The panel was not re-surveyed against the current implementation; doing so is deferred to future work. Fifteen evaluators completed the questionnaire (14 Likert items, 3 open questions); fourteen consented to their responses being used as validation evidence (one declined and was excluded), exceeding the planned 6--10 target, yielding a headline agreement mean of 3.96/5 (10 items excluding the four superseded-design items above; 3.98/5 across all 14, Section~\ref{subsec:expertval}). Eight of the fourteen are affiliated with institutions unconnected to the author team (majority-independent), though recruitment remained convenience-sampled; the independent subset (3.90/5) rated modestly below the affiliated subset (4.05/5), a mild skew the panel composition mitigates. At least two affiliated evaluators (Acknowledgements) are MSc students in the authors' own department, a power relationship beyond simple affiliation this study does not separately quantify.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ============================================================

This work has presented a security-by-design methodology for centralized IoT ecosystems that addresses the security activation gap by integrating cybersecurity systematically from the earliest organizational stage. The methodology's four phases and seven stages produce documented, traceable artifacts; the entity-centric identity model provides technology-agnostic security specifications applicable to heterogeneous devices; and the dynamic Root of Trust process establishes verifiable cryptographic identity before any operational communication begins.

Empirical validation through a confirmatory case study \citep{yin2014case} applying the methodology to a DC Smart Microgrid IoT ecosystem yields four findings. First, all seven stages produced complete, traceable outputs with zero external guidance required (CSQ1). Second, the 14-parameter identity model across four entity types produced zero ambiguity (CSQ2). Third, the RoT process established verifiable mutual authentication: a PUF-based scheme (\texttt{esp32\_puflib} \citep{stanicek2022esp32puflib}) that reconstructs each node's identity from non-persistent physical characteristics rather than a stored key, combined with an asymmetric (ECDSA) device-authentication proof, a per-node symmetric server-authentication proof, and an ephemeral ECDH exchange giving the session key forward secrecy, validated end-to-end on real hardware for both nodes: SMG\_NODE\_01's detailed $N=30$ hard-reset trial (30/30 success, every trial on the first attempt, median handshake latency 1300\,ms, mean $1313 \pm 48$\,ms) is the headline round reported, and SMG\_NODE\_02's independent $N=30$ trial (30/30, also every trial on the first attempt) confirms the reliability profile generalizes across chips; this validation also found and fixed a miscalibrated local reliability gate that had originally rejected every attempt outright, a raw PUF measurement left unerased in NVS, a helper-data-manipulation oracle that an earlier form of the mitigation did not fully close, and a per-device node-identifier gap that blocked registering a second node at all (Limitations); only the helper-data-manipulation fix changes the derived key and required re-enrolling the device, in each of its two passes (CSQ3, Section~\ref{subsec:pufbench}). Fourth, the methodology achieved unweighted OWASP IoT Top~10 coverage of 6/10 items fully addressed and 4/10 partially addressed (I4 secure-update policy only; I7 data encrypted in transit but not yet at rest; I8 device management, a trade-off for a smaller on-device footprint; I10 physical hardening outside software scope) (CSQ4). This case study evidence is supplemented by an expert questionnaire (N=14, Section~\ref{subsec:expertval}) that returned a headline agreement mean of 3.96/5 (10 items; 3.98/5 across all 14), with the strongest agreement on methodology completeness and systematic security integration (B3, B14 means 4.36/5) and qualified agreement on cross-domain generalizability and threat-coverage weighting.

The methodology's parameterized, technology-agnostic structure is designed to support application across diverse IoT domains; cross-domain generalization is a design intent supported by expert opinion (B13 mean 3.79/5, with dissenting 2/5 and 1/5 ratings characterizing it as an unvalidated hypothesis) rather than a demonstrated property, and empirical validation in additional domains remains future work. Future work will extend validation to: ITER~2 (TRL~5+ with hardware security elements and signed-OTA implementation), cross-domain deployment (healthcare, industrial automation), an expanded expert evaluator pool with improved institutional independence, formal cryptographic protocol verification of the SMG's PUF-based authentication scheme, resolution of the identity-lifecycle gap (rotation, revocation, recovery), evaluation of per-node verifier wrapping or HSM-backed server storage to reduce central-server single-point-of-failure risk, and a lightweight chain-free sequencing mechanism to recover stronger Repudiation/I8 coverage without the on-device footprint of a full cryptographic session-chain audit.
